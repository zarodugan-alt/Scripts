# Stick-figure animation channel: research and launch strategy

**Prepared: 10 October 2026**  
**Your workflow:** AI images + selective AI video + AI narration + your own editing, clipping, stickers, zooms and sound effects.

## Executive recommendation

Build **a character-led explainer channel about the traps of modern digital life**: attention, AI, online persuasion, subscriptions and everyday decision-making.

A possible channel promise:

> “One tiny stickman tries to survive the internet. Every episode reveals a trick that costs him time, money or common sense.”

Start with **5–7 minute narrated episodes**, a recurring original character, and predominantly manually animated image layers. Use AI video only where it produces a better shot than simple keyframes. Extract two self-contained Shorts per episode.

**My first-video recommendation: “Why ‘One More Video’ Never Ends.”** It is easy to visualize, personally relevant, relatively inexpensive to produce, and creates a natural next episode about notifications or online persuasion. This is a strategic recommendation, not a claim that the topic is currently the highest-volume search term.

**Best more topical alternative: “Why AI Can Sound Right When It’s Wrong.”** The 2026 evidence for widespread AI use makes this timely, although it requires more careful technical research.

The central lesson: **simple artwork reduces production complexity; it does not remove the need for excellent writing, acting, timing and research.**

## 1. Scope, evidence and limitations

This is a public-source desk-research report, not an audit of private YouTube analytics. Research included official channel pages, publicly surfaced video titles and view counts, YouTube policy documents, YouTube’s animation research, and Pew research on AI adoption.

Important distinctions:

- **Observed:** channel descriptions, surfaced titles, approximate public views, published survey findings and policies.
- **Interpretation:** why a format may attract clicks, sustain attention or encourage sharing. These are hypotheses, not proven causal explanations.
- **Recommendation:** proposed scripts, timings, topic rankings and production allocations for your channel.

I did not directly watch and timestamp every video, retrieve complete transcripts, or obtain creators’ retention curves. Therefore the structural breakdowns below are adaptable format models, not claims about exact editing intervals in particular videos.

Public pages can be cached; view counts change. Relative upload ages below are reproduced as displayed, not converted into asserted publication dates. There is no verified October 2026 search-volume ranking in this report. Broad animation demand is not proof of demand for a particular stickman topic. Earnings estimates from third-party calculators are intentionally excluded.

Assumptions: English-language YouTube, mainly teens and adults rather than preschool viewers, solo production, and long-form as the main product. If your intended audience is Hindi/Hinglish, localize scenarios and jokes rather than translating word-for-word.

## 2. The competitive landscape: six different formats

### A. Alan Becker — visual storytelling and action

**Verified identity:** Becker describes his work as stick-figure animation taking place on a computer desktop, without dialogue but with humor and emotion. [8](https://www.youtube.com/@alanbecker)

**Style model:** clean silhouettes, characters differentiated visually, understandable goals, and environments that participate in the action.

**Structural model to study:** character encounters a rule → experiments → discovers a possibility → the possibility creates trouble → an earlier discovery enables the resolution.

**Likely appeal:** viewers can understand stakes visually; a familiar object or system becomes surprising; the story offers both action and a payoff. Lack of dialogue can reduce language barriers, though comprehension is never automatic.

**Useful lesson:** make abstract systems physical. A notification can tug a character’s sleeve; a subscription can become a small creature eating coins.

**Poor fit for your initial workflow:** precise combat, hand-object contact, multi-character continuity and long action sequences. Simple character design should not be mistaken for simple animation.

**Recommendation:** borrow cause-and-effect storytelling, not Becker’s characters, recognizable scenes or production ambition.

### B. Casually Explained — comedy-first explanation

**Verified identity:** public descriptions characterize the channel as dry, sarcastic explanations of everyday subjects using rudimentary drawings. Its channel page features “Is She Into You?”, “Levels of Wealth,” and “Red Flags.” [3](https://youtube.fandom.com/wiki/Casually_Explained) [1](https://www.youtube.com/casuallyexplained)

**Style model:** the narrator appears reasonable while the example or conclusion becomes increasingly absurd. Minimal visuals leave room for the joke.

**Structural model:** familiar question → apparently useful explanation → increasingly revealing examples → reversal → callback.

**Likely appeal:** social recognition (“I know someone like this”), quotable jokes, and the pleasure of anticipating a running gag.

**Useful lesson:** the picture need not repeat the narration. Let the narration say, “He has everything under control,” while the character’s desk burns metaphorically beneath 40 browser tabs.

**Risk:** deadpan without good writing becomes flat. An AI voice cannot rescue a sentence with no surprise.

**Fit:** high for images and editing; medium-to-high writing difficulty.

### C. The Paint Explainer — modular curiosity explainers

**Verified identity and workflow:** its own description says narration uses generic AI text-to-speech presets, AI assists research/writing/polishing, and drawings are made by a human artist, with limited supplementary AI use. It explicitly distinguishes its production from fully automated workflows. [2](https://www.youtube.com/@ThePaintExplainer/about)

**Style model:** accessible illustrated explanations, compact subtopics, explicit labels and curiosity-led packaging. A secondary roundup documents topics including misconceptions, marketing tricks and disturbing planets. [6](https://theawesomer.com/tag/the-paint-explainer/)

**Structural model:** a broad promise → several individual questions → concise explanation and example for each → progressively stronger or stranger material.

**Likely appeal:** the viewer can understand the promised value immediately; each section offers another opportunity for discovery.

**Useful lesson:** deliver answers early, rather than keeping everything behind “wait until the end.”

**Risk:** interchangeable encyclopedia scripts, unsupported claims, repetitive visuals and an absence of personality.

**Fit:** high. This is the closest operational reference for your proposed workflow, but its disclosed human artwork is a meaningful difference.

### D. GradeAUnderA — personality-led commentary

**Verified identity:** public descriptions identify satirical rants/commentary and recurring drawn characters used to express reactions. [5](https://gradeaundera.fandom.com/wiki/GradeAUnderA)

**Structural model:** grievance → example → counterargument → exaggerated comparison → final verdict or punchline.

**Likely appeal:** the audience connects with a recognizable point of view, not merely information.

**Useful lesson:** give your narrator opinions about trivial situations: “A free trial is an appointment your future self never agreed to attend.”

**Risk:** manufactured outrage, repetitive shouting, factual overstatement or hostility toward individuals. These are not necessary ingredients of engaging commentary.

**Fit:** visually easy; performance-dependent. A neutral synthetic delivery may struggle with a script written as an angry live rant.

### E. Pencilmation — visual slapstick

**Verified identity:** the official channel describes a creator-versus-creation premise, recurring characters including Pencilmate and Pencilmiss, and a global team of artists. [1](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCUAL--p3qAa27luR0IYbjZA)

**Structural model:** small desire → obstacle → attempted fix → fix makes it worse → reversal.

**Likely appeal:** readable physical problems, escalating consequences and accessible visual humor.

**Useful lesson:** escalate a problem rather than repeating it. One notification interrupts him; the next blocks the doorway; the last becomes his landlord.

**Risk:** visual comedy depends on precise timing and poses. AI-generated motion may look active without communicating the joke.

**Fit:** medium for isolated gags, low for a complete beginner attempting continuous slapstick.

### F. Stickman survival / what-if channels — premise-led explanation

**Observed examples:** Stickman Survival Lab describes animated explanations of sinking cars, freezing water and other emergencies; STICKMAN’S WHAT IF? presents unusual science questions; Stickman Survives describes AI-assisted ancient survival stories. These descriptions establish positioning, not demonstrated commercial success. [2](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC9Ix-HdTaBMCNwfL_5mgHdw) [3](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCITIreMAjRP0Vg0LP-tlpDA) [6](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCQ6dUk7UCR_R5qgZ93kCvDw)

**Structural model:** dangerous situation → choices → consequence → explanation → resolution.

**Likely appeal:** immediate stakes and a natural “what would I do?” question.

**Risk:** invented rescue techniques or misleading medical claims can harm viewers. Fictional animation does not make factual advice harmless.

**Fit:** technically suitable, but a heavier research burden than digital-life comedy. Not my first recommendation for your launch.

### Comparative decision

| Format | AI-image/editing fit | Main difficulty | Launch recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrated explainers | High | Research and originality | Best foundation |
| Relatable comedy | High | Jokes and delivery | Add as a differentiator |
| Opinion commentary | High | Credible, distinctive voice | Use selectively |
| Survival/science | High | Accuracy and safety | Consider after building research discipline |
| Silent slapstick | Medium | Poses and timing | Use short inserts |
| Cinematic fights | Low | Motion and continuity | Avoid initially |

These are my workflow judgments, not measured channel success scores.

## 3. What public performance actually tells us

### The Paint Explainer: a useful within-channel contrast

The retrieved channel page displayed approximately:

| Video | Displayed views | Displayed age |
|---|---:|---|
| The Most Guarded Places on Earth You Can’t Visit | 2.2M | 1 month |
| Every Extinct Animal That’s Returning | 297K | 1 month |
| Ancient Greek Myths That Turned Out to Be True | 321K | 1 month |
| The Most Guarded Places… Part 2 | 571K | 9 days |
| The Most Guarded Places… Part 3 | 155K | 2 days |

Source: retrieved official channel page. [2](https://www.youtube.com/@ThePaintExplainer/about)

The first guarded-places video showed roughly 6.9–7.4 times the views of the two other roughly month-old uploads. **Interpretation:** “restricted access + real places + you cannot go there” may be a particularly strong curiosity package for this audience. That is a hypothesis, not proof: thumbnails, distribution, upload timing and audience history may explain part of the difference.

Do **not** call Parts 2 and 3 failures based on this table: they had much less time to accumulate views. Nor does the original’s success prove a new channel should duplicate its title.

### Casually Explained: more than one topic can work

The retrieved page displayed “Social Status” and “The Dunning-Kruger Effect” at approximately 2.5M views each, both shown as one month old. “Our Tech Overlords” displayed about 1.2M at three months. Older popular videos included “Is She Into You?” at over 22M and “Levels of Wealth” at about 13M. [1](https://www.youtube.com/casuallyexplained)

**Interpretation:** social identity and self-recognition are promising ingredients. However, these are results from an established comedy brand—not a forecast for a new AI-assisted channel. Older lifetime totals demonstrate longevity, not present-day upload velocity.

### Why some videos spread and others do not

Use this as a diagnostic model, not a formula for the recommendation system:

**Understandable promise → click → immediate confirmation → accumulating value → satisfying payoff → reason to share or return.**

| Works when… | Fails when… |
|---|---|
| The viewer recognizes a specific problem | The topic is “interesting facts” with no clear audience |
| A title opens one concrete question | A title promises the entire universe in six minutes |
| The first scene demonstrates the premise | A logo and channel introduction delay the video |
| Every segment advances an explanation or story | New images merely restate the narration |
| A character makes choices with consequences | The character stands beside bullet points throughout |
| Humor creates recognition | Stickers and sound effects substitute for jokes |
| The answer is useful or surprising | The reveal is something the viewer already knew |
| The next episode serves the same viewer | Uploads alternate between horror, finance and children’s games |

A viral video can also attract the wrong audience for the next upload. For a channel, repeat interest matters more than one disconnected spike.

## 4. Recent trend signals—and how much to trust them

### Signal 1: independent animation has a substantial audience

YouTube’s April 2026 animation report says 63% of surveyed 14–24-year-old animation fans watch YouTube-created animated series weekly or more, and 61% like independent YouTube series as much as or more than major-studio series. **The underlying survey was conducted in April 2025 among 358 U.S. online Gen Z animation fans**, not the entire public. [4](https://blog.youtube/culture-and-trends/independent-animation-trends-report/)

**Implication:** recurring original characters are worth testing. This does not prove every animated explainer will work or that expensive motion is necessary.

### Signal 2: AI production is no longer much of a differentiator

YouTube’s January 2026 CEO letter says more than one million channels used its AI creation tools daily on average in December, while explicitly discussing low-quality AI content as a concern. This concerns YouTube’s own tools, not every external AI tool. [9](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/the-future-of-youtube-2026/)

**Implication:** do not build the value proposition around “made with AI.” Build it around original scenarios, a recognizable character and strong editorial judgment.

### Signal 3: AI itself is a relevant subject

Pew’s February 2026 survey of 5,119 U.S. adults, published in June, found 49% reported using AI chatbots compared with 33% in 2024. Question wording changed across those waves, which should temper exact growth comparisons. [1](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/06/PI_2026.06.17_Americans-and-AI_REPORT.pdf)

**Implication:** AI confidence, verification, work and everyday dependence are plausible timely subjects. Adoption is evidence of relevance, not YouTube search demand.

### Signal 4: simplicity does not mean automation is enough

The Paint Explainer’s own production disclosure includes human artists and human intervention. YouTube’s monetization policy excludes mass-produced or repetitive content with insufficient originality. [2](https://www.youtube.com/@ThePaintExplainer/about) [1](https://support.google.com/youtubecreatorstudio/answer/1311392?hl=en-GB)

**Implication:** a cohesive visual system is good; replacing nouns in essentially the same script repeatedly is not a strong business strategy.

### What I would not claim

- That stickman channels are currently growing faster than every other format.
- That adding a zoom every three seconds improves every video.
- That a specific AI voice guarantees retention.
- That 20 topics can be ranked by guaranteed virality.
- That a channel’s public views reveal its earnings, profit or production budget.

## 5. Recommended original style

**Working positioning:** “A tiny person versus modern-life systems.” Check name and trademark availability before choosing a channel name.

**Character:** one simple black-outline figure with a distinctive teal rectangular backpack. Give him a behavioral flaw: he always chooses the easiest immediate option, then tries to explain away the consequences. Create your own proportions and expressions rather than tracing competitors.

**Narrator:** observant, amused and slightly sympathetic. The character is not stupid; he is recognizably human.

**Visual vocabulary:**

- Warm off-white background; charcoal lines; teal for the protagonist; orange/red for alerts.
- One important action per composition.
- Large shapes that remain legible on a phone.
- Reusable sets: desk, couch, checkout, phone interface, imaginary control room.
- Original fictional interfaces unless a real product is essential to the explanation.
- Abstract forces become objects: attention as tokens, a deadline as a shrinking doorway, a subscription as a coin-eating pet.

**Episode engine:** the character wants something simple → a system exploits or complicates the choice → attempts fail → the mechanism becomes clear → a modest practical response → comic callback.

The mechanism must vary meaningfully across episodes. A consistent character is branding; identical arguments and scenes are repetition.

## 6. Hook and script structure

I interpret “kick structure” as your opening hook, pacing, re-hooks and payoff.

### A practical hook: situation + contradiction + consequence

Weak: “Today we’ll discuss the effects of social media on society.”

Better: “He opened his phone to check the weather. Forty minutes later, he knows how to restore a medieval sword. He still doesn’t know if it’s raining.”

Why it is worth testing: it presents a visual situation, a recognizable behavior and an unanswered question. It does not need to claim a medical diagnosis.

Other original hook patterns:

- **Choice trap:** “Both buttons say you have a choice. Only one is easy to find.”
- **Escalation:** “His first subscription cost three dollars. Then it made friends.”
- **Confidence gap:** “The AI answer was clear, detailed and beautifully formatted. Unfortunately, the source did not exist.”
- **Identity reversal:** “He researched productivity for two hours. This was his most productive way to avoid working.”

### Suggested 5–7 minute architecture

Timings are starting design targets, not rules derived from competitor retention data.

| Time | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:15 | Show the problem | Weather check becomes an absurd detour |
| 0:15–0:35 | Establish the question | Why is stopping harder than starting? |
| 0:35–1:20 | First mechanism + visible example | There is no obvious stopping point |
| 1:20–2:10 | Character tries a weak fix | He promises himself “just one more” |
| 2:10–3:10 | Complication | The next item always offers a new possibility |
| 3:10–4:15 | Reframe | Personal intentions interact with interface design and context |
| 4:15–5:30 | Concrete responses | Add a stopping cue, change access friction, choose a purpose |
| 5:30–6:00 | Payoff and callback | He finally checks the weather—by opening the curtain |

If your researched material supports only four strong minutes, make a four-minute video. Do not inflate it to meet a template.

### The reusable mini-segment

**Claim → concrete scene → explanation → consequence → joke or useful takeaway.**

For example: explain that a missing endpoint can make a stopping decision less obvious; depict a character climbing stairs that keep extending; then let him install a door. The analogy aids understanding but does not replace evidence.

### Re-hooks that earn attention

Use a new problem, not merely “But wait!”

- “Removing notifications helped. Until he started checking without them.”
- “He found the cancel button. It was the beginning of the process.”
- “The answer was wrong. The dangerous part was how easy it was to trust.”

Close questions you open. Constantly delaying the answer can feel manipulative.

### Humor and voice direction

Use visual contradiction, escalating inconvenience and callbacks. Avoid copying catchphrases or a creator’s recognizable persona.

Start around 135–155 spoken words per minute, then adjust after listening. Generate a few sentences at a time so you can fix emphasis without regenerating the entire narration. Write short sentences and insert the pause before the punchline in your editor. A restrained delivery is usually easier to direct than nonstop synthetic excitement.

## 8. First-video production blueprint

### Title and promise

**Recommended title:** Why “One More Video” Never Ends

**Audience promise:** a funny, understandable explanation of how small decisions become a long scrolling session—and a few practical ways to create an endpoint.

**Alternative packaging to test:**

- “You Opened Your Phone for 10 Seconds…” — more narrative, less explanatory.
- “The Problem With ‘Just One More’” — broader, but may be too vague.

**Thumbnail:** one original character halfway inside a giant phone, with a tiny clock outside it. At most two or three words: “ONE MORE.” Do not add the entire title, multiple arrows and six unrelated app logos.

### Original opening sample: approximately 30 seconds

> “This is Pip. Pip opened his phone to check the weather. One useful video later, he learned how to season a pan. Then how to survive on Mars. Then why a stranger was furious about a chair. Forty minutes passed. Pip still didn’t know if it was raining. He doesn’t need a lecture about willpower. He needs to understand why ‘one more’ never tells him when to stop.”

“Pip” is a placeholder character name; check availability before using it as branding.

| Beat | Visual | Editing / sound |
|---|---|---|
| Introduce the character | Clean desk, phone, small clock | No logo intro |
| Weather intention | Cloud icon above phone | Light tap |
| Pan detour | Pan drops into frame | Small clink |
| Mars detour | Helmet pops onto head | Short air hiss |
| Chair argument | Tiny furious chair silhouette | Brief comic squeak |
| Time jump | Clock advances; room light shifts | Sound drops out |
| Reveal the problem | Wide shot, character in identical pose | Hold before the punchline |
| Transition | Phone becomes a long corridor | Narration leads into explanation |

### Research before finishing the script

Find credible sources for stopping cues, habitual checking, interface choices and any specific behavioral claim. Separate correlation from causation. Avoid “your dopamine is broken,” “this cures addiction,” and unsupported universal claims about attention spans.

Use an evidence sheet:

| Proposed claim | Reliable source | What it actually supports | Script wording / caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interface removes an endpoint | Relevant research or product documentation | Design feature vs proven behavioral effect | Match wording to evidence |
| Notifications prompt checking | Relevant study | Population and study setting | Avoid generalizing to everyone |
| A tactic might help | Research or credible guidance | Benefit and limits | Present as an experiment, not a cure |

The finished episode should explain a small number of mechanisms accurately, not decorate common advice with neuroscience terminology.

### Asset budget

Suggested initial scope: 12–16 reusable character poses, 5 basic sets, 15–20 props, 25–40 meaningful shot changes and zero to three short AI-video inserts. Several shots can reuse one background with new poses and camera framing.

For roughly six minutes, expect about 750–900 words depending on pauses. Estimate **12–24 hours for a first production**, including research and learning; this is a planning range, not an industry benchmark. Track actual hours and generation retries to improve the estimate.

## 9. Your AI-assisted production workflow

### Step 1 — Validate the package before making assets

Write a one-sentence audience promise, three titles and three tiny thumbnail sketches. Ask: does each describe the same video? Can someone understand it without knowing the channel?

Search YouTube manually for the topic. Examine recent uploads, not just famous all-time hits. If every result uses the same framing, find a different scenario or question rather than swapping in synonyms.

### Step 2 — Research, outline and fact-check

Use AI to suggest questions, alternatives and possible gaps. Do not treat generated citations as evidence. Open the sources, record dates, and write your own explanation. Keep factual narration separate from invented comic events.

### Step 3 — Build the script and shot list together

Use columns for narration, visible action, text, source and sound. If a sentence has no useful picture or no narrative purpose, revise it. If the visual already communicates the joke, remove unnecessary explanation.

### Step 4 — Lock the character design

Create reference poses and a small style guide. Keep head shape, stroke weight, limb proportions, backpack and palette consistent. Separate backgrounds, character layers and props.

Illustrative image prompt:

> “Single original 2D stick-figure character with black rounded lines, circular head, two dot eyes and a small teal rectangular backpack. Warm off-white background. Flat vector-like drawing, no gradients, no shadows, no lettering. Full body, readable silhouette. Pose: leaning cautiously toward a large orange button. Maintain the supplied character reference exactly.”

Generate one pose or scene at a time. Add all interface text in the editor. Inspect and correct extra limbs, changing accessories and inconsistent scale. Reference inputs can help but do not guarantee continuity.

### Step 5 — Animate images manually by default

For this style, try a starting allocation of **75–90% keyframed layers/pose swaps and 10–25% or less generative video**. This is an efficiency recommendation, not a necessary ratio. Zero AI-video footage is acceptable.

Use keyframes for position, scale and rotation; swap poses for clear actions. A clean three-pose gesture can communicate more effectively than a five-second clip of unstable motion.

Use image-to-video only for bounded actions, such as a short walk or a prop reaction. Avoid regenerating an entire scene when the only needed motion is a camera push.

Illustrative motion prompt:

> “Animate the supplied 2D character. Locked camera, unchanged plain background. Character leans forward, raises one arm and pauses. Maintain exact silhouette, backpack color and line thickness. No new objects, no text, no camera movement, no morphing. Short action with a stable final pose.”

Accept a shot only if continuity survives frame-by-frame inspection. For a stubborn shot, use keyframes instead of spending credits indefinitely.

### Step 6 — Direct the AI narration

Choose a licensed generic voice that can sound curious, restrained and amused. Do not clone another creator or imply that a celebrity narrated your video.

Create a pronunciation list. Generate short segments, listen in sequence, remove awkward pauses and match the edit to the actual audio duration. Keep the narrator’s identity consistent across episodes, while varying performance with the situation.

### Step 7 — Use effects to communicate

- **Zoom:** reveals a detail or lands a reaction.
- **Sticker:** represents a new consequence, not random decoration.
- **Whoosh:** accompanies a meaningful transition, not every sentence.
- **Impact:** emphasizes a collision or surprising cost.
- **Silence:** gives the audience time to notice the joke.
- **Caption:** emphasizes a key word; it should not duplicate every label on screen.

Check the mix on a phone speaker. Narration must remain intelligible beneath music and effects. Use appropriately licensed sound libraries.

### Step 8 — Export and quality-check

Check at phone size: readable text, safe framing, no accidental brand/logo distortions, consistent voice, no clipping or unwanted silence. Ensure the ending delivers the title’s promise.

For Shorts, recompose vertically instead of blindly cropping. Each Short needs its own premise, explanation and payoff; a random excerpt with no setup is weak promotion.

### Tool categories, not a shopping list

Use one image generator with reference support, one optional image-to-video system, one commercially licensed TTS service and one editor. CapCut, DaVinci Resolve, Premiere or equivalent editors can be considered according to your device and skills. Check current pricing, regional availability and commercial terms before paying; this report does not benchmark tools or guarantee plan features.

Budget for **accepted output**, not advertised price per generation. A cheap tool that needs ten retries may cost more time and money than a reliable manual edit.

## 10. Monetization, disclosure and rights

YouTube renamed “repetitious content” to **“inauthentic content”** in July 2025, clarifying that repetitive or mass-produced content is ineligible for monetization. This is not a blanket ban on AI narration or AI-assisted creation. Eligibility depends on the channel and content meeting the applicable policies; no workflow guarantees approval. [1](https://support.google.com/youtubecreatorstudio/answer/1311392?hl=en-GB)

Original research, substantive explanations, original storylines and purposeful visual communication should be the foundation. Adding stickers or zooms to generic copied scripts does not automatically make them original.

YouTube’s disclosure guidance distinguishes realistic, meaningfully AI-generated or altered content from clearly non-realistic animation and minor production assistance. It says non-realistic AI animation generally does not require disclosure solely for that reason, while realistic misleading depictions do. Review the actual video and current upload questions, including its audio, rather than assuming every AI use has the same requirement. YouTube also says disclosure itself does not limit monetization eligibility. [1](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)

Recommended practices:

- Describe your AI-assisted workflow honestly in the channel description.
- Use commercially permitted voices, fonts, music, images and effects; save license records.
- Do not lift competitor scripts, trace distinctive characters or replicate their thumbnails nearly verbatim.
- Label fictional experiments as fictional; never invent results from a test you did not run.
- Verify science, consumer and financial claims; avoid unsupported diagnoses and universal promises.
- Do not assume a cartoon is necessarily aimed at children. Assess the intended audience honestly when configuring the upload.

## 11. Launch and measurement plan

### Four-week pilot

**Week 1:** style guide, reference poses, research and episode #1. Finish a rough narrated animatic before spending heavily on video generation.

**Week 2:** publish #1; create two self-contained Shorts from different useful moments; record early audience questions.

**Week 3:** publish #6, “Why Every App Wants Your Attention,” with a stronger opening informed by the first episode.

**Week 4:** publish #3, the free-trial episode. Review all three as a related series rather than treating one upload as a verdict.

This is a proposed learning schedule, not a promise that three videos establish product-market fit. Slow the schedule if accuracy or editing suffers.

### A research sheet for ongoing competitor validation

For 5–10 relevant channels, record title, URL, upload date, duration, public views and topic. Compare videos of similar age and format. Exclude Shorts and compilations when comparing ordinary long-form episodes.

An approximate outlier ratio is:

**Video views ÷ median views of comparable recent uploads on that channel.**

Use comparable age windows whenever possible. Without historical snapshots, label the ratio approximate. A single small channel getting one large video is a lead to investigate, not proof of a dependable niche.

### Metrics and their interpretation

| Signal | Possible explanation | Next experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Impressions but weak CTR relative to similar traffic | Unclear or unappealing packaging | Simplify title/thumbnail promise |
| Clicks followed by an opening drop | Mismatch, slow setup or weak narration | Deliver the premise immediately |
| Drop during a repeated middle section | Redundancy or unclear explanation | Cut material or change the example |
| Spikes around a visual demonstration | Interest—or confusion causing rewatches | Inspect the scene before interpreting |
| Reasonable viewing but few return viewers | Topic works; channel promise may be unclear | Publish a closely related follow-up |
| Many Shorts views, little long-form interest | Different viewer intent | Improve topic alignment; do not assume a funnel |

Do not use a universal CTR or retention threshold to declare success. Traffic source, duration, audience familiarity and sample size matter. Check at consistent intervals such as 48 hours, 7 days and 28 days, but avoid treating tiny early samples as decisive. Change one important variable at a time where practical.

## 12. Final decision

**Yes—your workflow is suitable for launching this kind of channel.** The strongest route is not “AI generates a whole cartoon, then I add effects.” It is:

> **You choose the insight and write the story → AI helps create assets and narration → you control continuity, timing, humor, accuracy and the final edit.**

Start with “Why ‘One More Video’ Never Ends.” Give the protagonist an immediately recognizable problem, explain a few mechanisms carefully, and deliver a satisfying ending. Then make two related episodes before judging the format.

Your defensible advantage is not the stick figure or the AI voice. It is **a consistent point of view, an original recurring character, verified explanations and jokes that belong to your channel.**
