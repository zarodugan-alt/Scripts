# WHY “ONE MORE VIDEO” NEVER ENDS
## Episode 01 • A third-person stick-figure comedy explainer

**Script date:** 10 October 2026  
**Format:** One narrator; no spoken character dialogue; original fictional story with researched explanations.  
**Target finished runtime:** 20:00, including deliberate visual pauses and the final end card. Timing is a production target, not a measured voiceover duration.  
**Audience:** General teen/adult audience.  
**Tone:** Warm, observant, dryly funny. Never scolding.  
**Character:** Pip, an original stick figure with a teal backpack, a small apartment, and tremendous confidence in plans he has not yet attempted.

## READ THIS BEFORE RECORDING — NOT NARRATION

Only text between **NARRATION** and **END NARRATION** is spoken. Section headings, visual directions, source references and the appendix are not spoken.

The narrator tells Pip’s story entirely in third person. There are no direct-to-viewer instructions, voiced inner thoughts or first-person character speeches. On-screen labels are silent.

**Performance:** Conversational rather than announcer-like. Smile slightly at the absurd details, but do not announce jokes. Give sincere moments room. The comedy should feel as if the narrator noticed something funny, not as if the narrator is waiting for applause.

**Visual language:** Warm off-white, charcoal outlines, teal for Pip, orange for alerts. Most shots need only a background, a character pose, one prop and a camera move. The feed conveyor belt is a metaphor, not a literal reconstruction of a platform’s software. Use fictional interfaces.

**Music:** Light rhythmic opening; curious middle; quieter emotional turn; playful resolution. No ominous “brain damage” music. Brief silence often works better than another sound effect.

**Editorial accuracy:** Pip’s experiences and all displayed personal times are fictional. They are not experiment results or population averages. The story offers practical experiments, not a treatment protocol. Fact-check notes and evidence limitations appear after the script.

# THE SCRIPT

## SCENE 01 — THE WEATHER CHECK

**Animation:** Pip stands beside the apartment door wearing his backpack. A folded umbrella sits on a hook. He takes out his phone. Show the time, then a simple fictional feed. Each new topic drops a cheap, reusable prop onto him: frying pan, space helmet, tiny chair. Keep the first image moving immediately; no opening logo.

**NARRATION**

Pip needs to know whether it is raining.

This should be a short investigation. There is a window approximately one Pip away.

But the phone is already in his hand, and apparently that makes it the nearest weather station.

Before he opens the forecast, a friend’s video catches his eye. A man is restoring a filthy frying pan.

Pip respects the process.

The next video explains how a person could survive on Mars. This seems worth knowing. His rent has been going up.

Then somebody is furious about a chair.

Pip does not know the chair. He does not know the man. But the man has made several serious accusations about lumbar support, and Pip feels the chair deserves a fair hearing.

Forty minutes later, the pan is clean, Mars remains difficult, and the chair has divided a community.

Pip still does not know if it is raining.

He checks the time with the expression of a man who has just discovered a hidden charge on his own afternoon.

He never decided to spend forty minutes here.

He decided to watch one video.

Then he made that same tiny decision several more times, without ever meeting the full-sized decision they had become.

Now he wants his afternoon back.

Unfortunately, his first plan is to use the phone to research how to stop using the phone.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip searches. A tile appears: “STOP WASTING TIME — COMPLETE 47-PART SERIES.” He reaches for it. Freeze before the tap. Title appears briefly over the existing scene.

**Evidence anchor:** Fictional setup; no numerical behavioral claim.

## SCENE 02 — A VERY SMALL PURCHASE

**Animation:** Pip lays out a modest afternoon plan: send an email, wash a bowl, leave the apartment. A conveyor belt carries little video cards across the desk. Each card removes one small time token from a jar. Do not turn the scene into a busy infographic.

**NARRATION**

The strange part is that Pip has plans.

Not heroic plans. He is not supposed to climb a mountain or invent a cleaner source of energy.

He needs to send one email, wash one bowl, and leave the house before leaving the house becomes tomorrow’s ambition.

A full hour of random videos sounds expensive compared with that.

One short video sounds almost free.

That is the deal Pip keeps accepting. Not the whole afternoon. Just this small piece of it.

And each piece arrives with its own excuse.

This one is useful. This one is short. This one has a dog carrying a shopping bag, which is practically public service.

Some of these videos really are enjoyable. The dog is doing excellent work.

The problem is not that Pip likes entertainment. It is that he keeps extending a break without deciding whether the longer break is still what he wants.

He would notice if somebody asked for his entire afternoon at the door.

It is harder to notice when the afternoon leaves in tiny parcels.

Eventually he feels bored with the feed. Surely this is the natural place to stop.

Instead, he swipes to find something less boring.

For Pip, a good video becomes a reason to continue. A bad video becomes a reason to find a better one.

His exit has developed a small administrative problem.

Both doors lead back inside.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip opens two doors labeled “GOOD VIDEO” and “BAD VIDEO.” Both return him to the same chair. A tiny dog delivers the chair’s shopping.

**Evidence anchor:** Pip-specific comic interpretation, not a universal model of reward or addiction.

## SCENE 03 — THE MISSING LAST PAGE

**Animation:** A newspaper ends. A book closes. A finite playlist reaches its last item. Contrast these with a paper feed extending beyond frame. Pip tries to find the bottom; an ordinary roller supplies more paper. One loop is enough.

**NARRATION**

A newspaper has a last page.

A book has a back cover.

Even a packet of biscuits eventually provides a clear, if emotionally difficult, conclusion.

An endless feed does not provide the same kind of finish line.

More content loads as Pip moves. Where autoplay is enabled, another video may begin without a fresh decision to press play.

Those features are convenient. They can also make continuing easier than stopping.

The National Academies describes infinite scrolling, autoplay and recommendations as features that can encourage people to spend more time on a platform.

That does not mean a phone can force Pip to stay.

It means the design changes where the effort sits.

Continuing can take one familiar movement. Stopping requires him to notice what he is doing, remember what he meant to do, and interrupt it.

On paper, that sounds simple.

So does putting away laundry. Pip has a chair that disputes the evidence.

He tries reaching the bottom of the feed anyway.

There is no useful bottom to reach. New items keep arriving, like a waiter who takes away an empty plate and immediately replaces it with another starter.

Pip has been waiting for the experience to announce that he is finished.

The experience is very good at announcing something else.

There is more.

And more is not the same thing as a reason to stay.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** The waiter delivers a single pea on an enormous plate, followed by another plate. Pip gently blocks the third with one finger.

**Evidence anchor:** A1. Physical analogies illustrate design; they do not establish a measured effect size.

## SCENE 04 — THE FEED TAKES NOTES

**Animation:** Behind the conveyor belt, show a plainly fictional sorting room. Three cards: watched, skipped, liked. A sorter uses these to choose the next batch. No brain scanner, microphone spy gag or all-knowing villain.

**NARRATION**

Meanwhile, the feed is not simply pulling every video from a hat.

Recommendation systems use signals from activity to estimate what someone might engage with next.

TikTok’s public explanation, for example, describes signals such as viewing, liking, sharing and skipping. Its system predicts different interactions and ranks possible recommendations.

Other platforms work differently, and the details change.

But Pip does not need a computer science degree to understand the basic arrangement.

His actions provide clues. The system uses clues to make guesses.

He watches one pan restoration. More restoration videos may appear.

He spends time watching a ridiculous argument. That attention is also an observable action, even if his reason was disbelief rather than approval.

A system predicting interactions is not the same thing as a friend understanding his intentions.

It does not necessarily know whether a video left him delighted, annoyed, or wondering how adulthood produced a dispute about a chair.

And it does not need to know his entire personality to offer another plausible video.

Pip finds this slightly disappointing.

He preferred the idea that he had mysterious depths. Apparently one of those depths contains cookware.

Personalization can help him find genuinely useful things. That is part of its appeal.

But a good guess about what he might watch next is not automatically a good answer to what he wants from the next hour.

His afternoon plan is still sitting on the desk.

The sorter has not read it.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip offers the sorter his to-do list. It stamps the paper “INTERESTING PAPER VIDEO?” and returns it. Hold just long enough to read.

**Evidence anchor:** A2. Do not add exact algorithm weights or claim all platforms optimize only watch time.

## SCENE 05 — THE PHONE DID NOT EVEN RING

**Animation:** Pip silences three nonessential alerts. Preserve call and important-contact icons. He returns to his email, pauses, and reaches for the completely silent phone. Replay once from a wide angle; no fake scientific graph.

**NARRATION**

Pip decides the notifications are responsible.

This is a satisfying explanation because notifications have an off switch.

His unfinished responsibilities have been less cooperative.

Alerts can interrupt attention. In one laboratory study, receiving calls or texts disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even without people directly interacting with the phone.

So Pip turns off several alerts he does not need.

The room becomes quiet.

He opens his email.

Then he checks his phone.

Nothing happened. No sound. No vibration. Not even a suspicious flicker.

He has arrived to investigate an event that did not occur.

Checking can become a routine, not just a response to an alert. Observational research has documented people initiating phone interactions themselves, often as part of habitual checking.

That helps explain why silence alone is not a complete solution.

In one randomized study, a week of disabled notifications did not reduce participants’ screen time or checking frequency.

That does not make notification settings useless. It means removing one trigger may leave other reasons to check.

For Pip, one of those reasons is the moment just before an uncomfortable task.

His email is not especially difficult. He just cannot decide how to begin it without sounding strange.

The phone offers an immediate alternative.

A stranger has begun pressure-washing a driveway.

Pip is relieved. At last, somebody has a clear plan.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** On the laptop: “Hello,” then “Hi,” then “Hello,” again. On the phone, one extremely clean stripe appears on a driveway. Pip gives a solemn nod.

**Evidence anchor:** A3, A4, A5. The laboratory finding concerns a task, not a diagnosis or a universal level of impairment.

## SCENE 06 — THE BREAK THAT FORGOT ITS JOB

**Animation:** The email envelope expands into a large stage prop. Pip retreats into a cozy phone-shaped armchair. Later, shrink the envelope back to normal size while the room darkens slightly. Keep this section gentler and less cut-heavy.

**NARRATION**

This is where Pip’s problem becomes more human than a story about clever software.

Sometimes he scrolls because he wants a laugh.

Sometimes he is waiting for somebody.

And sometimes he does not want to feel the awkward little feeling waiting inside his next task.

The phone does not have to solve that feeling. It only has to offer somewhere else to look.

For a moment, it works.

There is no email while the driveway is being washed. There is only a driveway becoming the best version of itself.

A break can be worthwhile. Research on absorbed social media use includes people describing these moments as beneficial, not only regrettable.

But Pip’s particular break has lost track of its original job.

He meant to return feeling ready.

Instead, the email is still there, and now he is annoyed about the time he spent avoiding it.

That is not proof that he is lazy. It is a description of how this afternoon has gone.

Calling himself useless does not write the email either.

It mainly creates another unpleasant feeling, which the phone is delighted to provide a distraction from.

Pip looks at the bowl in the sink.

The bowl has not become more complicated. It has simply been waiting long enough to feel personal.

He needs a smaller first step.

Unfortunately, he initially chooses a much bigger promise.

Tomorrow, he will become a completely different person.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** A fantasy Pip appears in running shoes beside a sunrise and an immaculate desk. Real Pip looks down: he has one sock on. Let the contrast land without a sting.

**Evidence anchor:** A6 for the mixed experience of absorbed use. Avoidance, guilt and the email are characterization, not clinical explanations for everyone.

## SCENE 07 — TOMORROW’S PIP

**Animation:** Fantasy Pip performs impossible efficiency with simple pose swaps. Real Pip builds a chart, downloads a timer and rearranges icons. A wall calendar flips once, not across an implied transformation montage.

**NARRATION**

Tomorrow’s Pip is impressive.

Tomorrow’s Pip wakes up early, enjoys plain water, and answers messages without spending eight minutes deciding whether a full stop sounds angry.

Today’s Pip places enormous faith in this man.

He makes a rule: no more pointless scrolling.

It is a powerful sentence. It is also missing several useful details.

What counts as pointless? When does a planned break end? What happens when he opens the app for something useful and finds something else?

The rule answers none of these questions, but it looks excellent written in capital letters.

Next, Pip sets a screen-time limit.

When the reminder appears, he dismisses it so quickly that the reminder barely has time to introduce itself.

A prompt can create a chance to notice. It cannot guarantee what happens after that chance.

Pip has treated the warning like a speed bump on a road he still intends to drive down.

Then he reorganizes every app on his phone.

He spends a long time selecting a calming wallpaper.

The wallpaper is now calm. Pip has not yet joined it.

He has prepared beautifully for a new life while avoiding one small action in the current one.

This does not mean preparation is worthless.

It means his plan needs to survive the ordinary Pip who will actually use it.

Especially the version who is tired, mildly bored, and aware that somewhere a very dirty rug is becoming clean.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Real Pip tries to hand fantasy Pip the phone. The fantasy disappears. The phone drops neatly back into his own hand.

**Evidence anchor:** Reminder limitations align with A6; Pip’s failed attempts are fictional, not claims that limits or icon changes never help.

## SCENE 08 — NO, HIS BRAIN DOES NOT NEED A FACTORY RESET

**Animation:** A dramatic fictional video tile promises “RESET EVERYTHING.” A bottle labeled “DOPAMINE DRAIN CLEANER” appears as an obvious absurdity, then gets a large crossed-out mark. Switch to simple neutral labels: learning, motivation, reward. Never show invented brain scans or a measurable dopamine gauge.

**NARRATION**

While investigating his problem, Pip finds another explanation.

Apparently, his dopamine is broken.

This diagnosis comes from a video with flashing red arrows and a man pointing at a brain he almost certainly does not own.

The solution involves resetting everything, avoiding all enjoyment, and possibly purchasing a worksheet.

Pip likes the certainty. Certainty saves time, especially when it is inaccurate.

Dopamine is a chemical messenger involved in functions including motivation, learning and reward.

It is not a poison that builds up because somebody watched too many cooking videos.

Harvard Health has specifically criticized the literal idea of a dopamine fast. Avoiding enjoyable activities does not simply empty and refill a brain chemical like changing the oil in a car.

A break from a troublesome habit may still be useful.

That does not make the dramatic chemical explanation true.

Pip does not need to declare war on music, food, friends or every small thing that makes a Tuesday less like a Tuesday.

Nor does one lost afternoon establish that he has an addiction or a medical disorder.

His actual problem is specific enough to investigate without diagnosing his entire nervous system.

He keeps opening a feed without a clear purpose, continuing without an endpoint, and returning when he does not mean to.

That description is less exciting than a broken brain.

It is also considerably easier to make a practical plan for.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip removes an enormous “FACTORY RESET” lever from beside his head. It was attached with tape. He replaces it with a small paper checklist.

**Evidence anchor:** A7. No claims about measured dopamine levels, receptor damage or a guaranteed reset period.

## SCENE 09 — HE STARTS WITH THE MOMENT BEFORE

**Animation:** Three short moments: waiting for the kettle, confronting the email, sitting down after a tiring day. Pip makes small marks on paper. Then show a direct weather shortcut, the feed icon moved away, and the phone resting on a shelf while the laptop remains usable.

**NARRATION**

Pip starts by noticing when the detours begin.

Not recording every second of his life. He has already turned self-improvement into office work once today.

Just a few moments.

Waiting for the kettle. Pausing before the email. Sitting down tired and reaching for the easiest available thing.

The same phone is serving several different needs.

That suggests several small experiments, rather than one heroic ban.

For practical tasks, he tries going straight to the tool he needs instead of passing through a feed on the way.

A weather shortcut cannot promise him a better personality, but it can provide the weather.

He moves the feed app away from its usual position. Now his thumb occasionally lands on empty space.

This is not a cure. It is a small opportunity to notice the movement.

For the email, he puts the phone on a nearby shelf, still available for important calls, but no longer resting beside his hand like a very persuasive coaster.

Then he gives the email an embarrassingly small starting point.

Write one rough sentence.

Not a perfect message. Not proof that he has become organized. One sentence that can be edited.

He writes it.

It is ordinary. Nobody will frame it.

But the task finally exists outside his imagination, and he has something real to work with.

The phone remains on the shelf.

For once, the driveway will have to wash itself without supervision.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip types a clumsy but usable sentence, adjusts one word, and continues. Give the typing time to happen; no triumphant transformation music.

**Evidence anchor:** Practical experiments, not study-tested claims about this exact package or a promised outcome.

## SCENE 10 — A BREAK WITH AN ACTUAL EXIT

**Animation:** Pip draws a door onto the conveyor-belt set. Show a chosen finite queue, a timer and a physical next action: putting the kettle on. Use a miniature calendar to distinguish later attempts from the original afternoon. Pip keeps one genuinely useful video.

**NARRATION**

Next, Pip changes the break itself.

He does not ban entertainment. He gives it a shape.

On one attempt, he chooses a small set of videos in advance. On another, he sets a timer and decides what he will do when it rings.

Not simply stop.

Stand up. Put the phone down. Make tea.

The tea is not a neurological intervention. It is somewhere concrete to go next.

Where the controls are available, he tries disabling autoplay. He avoids treating an endless feed like a list he can eventually finish.

These choices will not suit every person or every app. Pip is testing which ones interrupt his particular routine without making useful phone tasks unnecessarily difficult.

There is research behind the broader idea that design can help people notice their use.

In a small study using a custom Twitter app, researchers tested features including reading-history labels, custom lists and time reminders. Participants reported less of the absorbed, mindless scrolling the researchers were studying.

That was one study, on a particular app, with forty-three participants. Not a universal recipe.

Still, it gives Pip a better question than whether he has enough willpower.

What would make the stopping point easier to see?

His first timer rings.

He reaches to dismiss it.

Then he notices the kettle waiting beside his cup.

He stands up.

A tiny achievement, unless the alternative was another forty minutes becoming unusually informed about somebody else’s furniture.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Pip starts the kettle and watches actual steam. A playful “LIVE” badge appears over it, then disappears. He smiles without reaching for the phone.

**Evidence anchor:** A6. Personal experiments illustrate the principle, not effects established for every listed tactic.

## SCENE 11 — THE EVENING THAT DOES NOT GO PERFECTLY

**Animation:** Later evening. Pip’s feed is open again. Use no red failure stamp. He stops, glances at the time, puts the phone down and resumes an ordinary activity. Show a friend’s message and one shared funny dog video to preserve the positive role of the phone.

**NARRATION**

A few days later, Pip scrolls longer than he meant to.

There is no dramatic explanation. He is tired. The videos are easy. The old routine is still familiar.

For a moment, he considers declaring the whole plan a failure.

This would be convenient. A failed plan requires no further maintenance.

Instead, he notices that the phone came back to its old place beside his hand, and the break began without an ending.

Useful information. Not a verdict on his character.

He puts it down now, rather than treating the rest of the evening as already lost.

He also notices that not every minute on his phone feels the same.

A proper conversation with a friend is different from refreshing an argument he does not care about.

A chosen comedy video is different from continuing through clips he barely remembers.

Minutes can help him notice patterns. They do not explain everything about what those minutes contained.

Pip still watches videos. He sends the shopping-bag dog to his friend. The dog remains a valuable member of society.

His aim is not to produce an impressively low number while becoming miserable in several new ways.

It is to have more say in how the afternoon goes.

And if phone use were repeatedly disrupting his sleep, work or relationships, or felt persistently unmanageable, getting help from a qualified professional would be reasonable.

A cartoon checklist is not a substitute for that.

Neither is an internet stranger confidently diagnosing his dopamine.

**END NARRATION**

**Silent beat:** Two simple choices appear: “CHOSEN BREAK” and “ACCIDENTAL DETOUR.” Pip places the same phone between them. The phone itself does not become a monster or a halo.

**Evidence anchor:** Context-sensitive framing; professional-support line is general guidance, not a diagnosis.

## SCENE 12 — THE WINDOW

**Animation:** Return to the exact opening composition. Umbrella on the hook; backpack on Pip. Reuse the original shot for the callback. This time, Pip looks up before opening a feed. End with rain, not a productivity montage.

**NARRATION**

The next afternoon, Pip is ready to leave the apartment.

The email has been sent. The bowl has been washed. Neither achievement attracted a documentary crew, but both are finished.

He takes out his phone to check the weather.

A familiar video waits nearby.

For a moment, the old route is available. One interesting thing, then another, then a surprisingly emotional dispute about a household object.

He could still take that route.

This time, he notices that it is a route, not the only possible next step.

That is the part his enormous promise missed.

He did not need to become tomorrow’s flawless Pip. He needed ordinary Pip to get a little more help at the moment a choice was disappearing into a habit.

A clearer purpose before opening.

A visible place to stop.

Something real to return to afterward.

None of it guarantees a perfect afternoon. It just gives him more chances to choose the afternoon he meant to have.

The feed will not run out of videos for his benefit.

There will always be another pan, another argument, another dog with a stronger work ethic than most adults.

The possibility of another good video is not proof that this moment needs one.

Pip locks the phone.

Then he remembers the window.

He pulls the curtain aside.

It is raining.

At last, a complete answer. No sequel required.

He takes his umbrella and leaves.

Behind him, the phone stays quiet.

For once, the last thing in the story is not one more video.

It is Pip getting on with his day.

**END NARRATION**

**Final silent payoff:** Outside, Pip opens the umbrella. It opens inside out. He looks at it, then at the phone-shaped outline in his pocket. After a short hesitation, he fixes the umbrella by hand. A shopping-bag dog passes him, perfectly prepared in a tiny raincoat. Pip steps aside respectfully.

**End card:** Hold a simple original drawing and the channel mark. No spoken subscription plea, autoplay gag or instruction to keep watching. If a next-video panel is used, label it unobtrusively as an optional related episode; do not obscure the ending.

# PRODUCTION AND FACT-CHECK APPENDIX — NOT SPOKEN

## A. Claim-to-source ledger

Sources were checked on 10 October 2026. Where a full-text fetch was restricted or incomplete, the available indexed source text is identified below. No claim depends on an inaccessible table, invented quotation or private analytics.

### A1 — Interface design and stopping points

**Source:** National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. *Social Media and Adolescent Health*, chapter 2, “How Social Media Work,” 2024. DOI: 10.17226/27396. [1](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27396/chapter/4)

**Checked material:** Retrieved chapter opening plus indexed chapter passages on recommendations, autoplay, infinite scrolling and persuasive design.

**Supports:** These features can encourage extended use; affordances influence but do not determine behavior.

**Used in:** Scene 03.

**Boundary:** The report focuses on adolescent health. The script uses its description of interface features, not a generalized adolescent health-effect estimate. No claim that infinite scrolling forces use or causes a particular disorder.

### A2 — Recommendations use behavioral signals

**Source:** TikTok, “Introduction to the TikTok recommendation system.” [2](https://www.tiktok.com/transparency/en/recommendation-system)

**Additional source:** TikTok Newsroom, “How TikTok recommends videos #ForYou.” [1](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)

**Checked material:** Detailed indexed text describing signals, prediction scores, ranking and interaction types. A direct fetch of the transparency page did not return usable text.

**Supports:** Watching, finishing, liking, sharing and skipping are among the kinds of activity used in recommendations. Multiple predictions and rules are involved.

**Used in:** Scene 04.

**Boundary:** This is platform self-description, not an independent audit. Watching does not reveal a person’s emotional reason for watching. The script does not assert exact weights, deterministic results, microphone surveillance or one algorithm shared by every platform.

### A3 — Notifications can disrupt task attention

**Study:** Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., and Yehnert, C. (2015). “The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification.” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, 41(4), 893–897. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100.

**Checked source:** Association for Psychological Science’s report of the experiment. [3](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/motr/youre-distracted-even-when-you-ignore-that-text.html)

**Supports:** Notifications disrupted performance on an attention-demanding laboratory task, including without direct phone interaction.

**Used in:** Scene 05.

**Boundary:** The script does not equate this task with driving, claim permanent attention damage, cite an exact recovery time, or infer a diagnosis.

### A4 — Checking need not begin with a notification

**Source:** London School of Economics, “We engage with our phones every five minutes, new study shows,” 3 December 2020; research by Maxi Heitmayer and Saadi Lahlou. [1](https://www.lse.ac.uk/news/latest-news-from-lse/l-december/we-engage-with-our-phones-every-five-minutes-new-study-shows)

**Checked material:** University summary describing user-initiated interactions and routine checking.

**Supports:** In the observed sample, many interactions were initiated by users rather than alerts.

**Used in:** Scene 05.

**Boundary:** The script deliberately omits the headline frequency and the sample’s 89% figure, avoiding their presentation as universal human behavior. Observation does not prove why each individual checked.

### A5 — Disabling notifications is not a guaranteed screen-time fix

**Source:** “Beyond the Buzz: Investigating the Effects of a Notification-Disabling Intervention on Smartphone Behavior and Digital Well-Being.” *Media Psychology*, published online 28 March 2024. DOI: 10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025. [2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025)

**Checked material:** Indexed abstract and discussion; direct page extraction returned only page navigation.

**Supports:** In a preregistered randomized trial with 205 participants, a one-week notification-disabling intervention did not affect objectively logged screen time or checking frequency. Perceived checking habit strength decreased; some other experiences differed.

**Used in:** Scene 05.

**Boundary:** One intervention over one week does not establish that notifications never matter or that disabling them never helps. The script explicitly preserves that distinction.

### A6 — Absorbed use, mixed experiences and design interventions

**Source:** Baughan, A., et al. (2022). “I Don’t Even Remember What I Read”: How Design Influences Dissociation on Social Media. *CHI ’22*. DOI: 10.1145/3491102.3501899. [2](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/dissociation.pdf)

**Checked material:** Directly retrieved paper abstract and introduction, including methods summary and findings; indexed discussion passages.

**Supports:** Forty-three U.S. participants used a custom Twitter client in a four-week study. Researchers examined normative dissociation and tested custom lists, reading-history labels, time-limit dialogs and usage statistics. They reported reduced normative dissociation with the interventions. Participants described both beneficial absorption and unwanted mindless use.

**Used in:** Scenes 06, 07 and 10; context for Scene 11.

**Boundary:** This was a small study of a particular client, not a trial of every proposed personal habit. “Absorbed, mindless scrolling” is an accessible summary of the studied experience, not a clinical diagnosis. The script does not assert that all social media use is harmful, that all absorption is bad, or that these interventions cure addiction.

### A7 — Dopamine detox claims

**Source:** Peter Grinspoon, MD. “Dopamine fasting: Misunderstanding science spawns a maladaptive fad.” Harvard Health Publishing, 26 February 2020. [1](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/dopamine-fasting-misunderstanding-science-spawns-a-maladaptive-fad-2020022618917)

**Checked material:** Indexed full article passages on dopamine’s functions and why a literal dopamine fast misrepresents the science.

**Supports:** Dopamine is a neurotransmitter involved in reward, motivation, learning and pleasure; abstaining from enjoyable activities is not literally fasting from or replenishing dopamine stores.

**Used in:** Scene 08.

**Boundary:** This is a physician-authored explanatory article, not a clinical trial of a treatment. The script avoids claiming that all behavioral breaks are ineffective, and does not assert Pip’s dopamine concentration or receptor condition.

## B. Pre-publication editorial review

### Story and POV

- **Opening promise:** One quick weather check becomes an unchosen forty-minute detour.
- **Central question:** Why does a sequence of tiny choices become a much larger commitment, and what can Pip change?
- **Causal progression:** Tiny extensions → missing endpoints → personalized suggestions → self-initiated checking → avoidance → failed grand promises → correction of pseudoscience → practical changes → imperfect follow-through → resolved opening.
- **Third-person check:** One narrator describes Pip and the evidence. There is no spoken character dialogue, direct viewer address or first-person self-help monologue.
- **Character arc:** Pip moves from blaming his character or looking for a dramatic reset to noticing specific situations and making workable adjustments.
- **Ending:** Answers the opening weather question and resolves the emotional question without pretending the habit is permanently cured.

### Comedy and repetition

- **Main callbacks:** The weather, restored cookware, disputed chair, reliable dog and unfinished email.
- **Escalation:** Absurd content detour → bureaucratic failure of the exit → grand reinvention → modest real action.
- **Target of jokes:** Familiar behavior, inflated promises and absurd situations. Not people with diagnosed conditions or difficulties with self-control.
- **Restraint:** Scenes 06 and 11 intentionally ease off the joke density to prevent the narrator sounding cruel or mechanically punchy.
- **No borrowed catchphrases:** Wording and fictional situations were composed for this episode; no competitor transcript was used as a template. This is not an exhaustive plagiarism-database check.

### Fact-check exclusions

The script intentionally does **not** claim:

- Human attention spans are now eight seconds.
- Every swipe produces a measured dopamine spike.
- Social media is neurologically identical to cocaine or gambling.
- A dopamine detox resets receptors in a day or a week.
- The algorithm can read minds or knows exactly what Pip feels.
- All platforms optimize only watch time.
- Turning off alerts always reduces checking.
- Every long viewing session is an addiction.
- A specific time limit, app arrangement or tea break works for everyone.
- Pip’s fictional improvement demonstrates a measured treatment effect.

### Production review

- Most scenes work with pose swaps, keyframed props, simple masks and a small number of reusable sets.
- The fantasy, conveyor belt and sorting room are visibly metaphorical.
- On-screen text should remain short and large enough for mobile viewing.
- Avoid constant zooms. Reserve close-ups for a new detail or a reaction.
- Keep source cards subtle; do not read URLs aloud.
- Put study links in the video description. If showing study findings on screen, retain qualifiers such as “one study” and “43 participants.”
- Record a scratch voiceover before generating final animated clips. Animation should follow the performed timing, not force unnatural speech to match pre-generated footage.
